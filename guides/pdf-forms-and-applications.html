Fillable PDF forms and applications

Permit, application and registration forms are the PDFs residents have to use to get something done. They are also where an untagged file does the most harm.

The Department of Justice's ADA Title II web rule excuses some older "preexisting" documents, but not ones that are currently used to apply for, get access to, or take part in a public entity's services. A form a resident fills in to apply for a permit or a service fits that description on its face. Your counsel decides how it applies to a given file; this page is not legal advice. The dates are in the deadline calculator.

What goes wrong for a screen-reader user

  • Fields with no names. A form field without a label or tooltip is announced as "edit text" with nothing else, so the user cannot tell the name field from the date field.
  • No tags, or a scrambled reading order, so instructions are read after the fields they explain, or not at all.
  • A scanned paper form. It is a picture: there is no text and there are no fields to fill in.
  • Tab order that jumps around, so keyboard users land in the wrong box.

A short check you can do in ten minutes

  1. Open the PDF and try to select a line of text. If you can't, it is a scan.
  2. Press Tab repeatedly. The focus should move through the fields in the order a person would fill them in.
  3. Hover over each field: it should show a tooltip that names it ("Applicant last name").
  4. Run the free browser check to see whether the file is tagged and has a title and language. It does not look at your field names for you.

What to do

Where a form is short, an online web form is often the better answer. Otherwise fix the source file, give every field a name and a sensible tab order, and tag the instructions. Forms are the files we price as complex pages (3 credits a page), because the fields have to be tied to their labels.
